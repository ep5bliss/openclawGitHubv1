# Start Here

If you read one page of this book, read this one.

You are standing in an aisle. A kid is asking for something. You have about ten seconds before this becomes a negotiation, and the box in your hand has roughly eighteen lines of numbers on the back of it.

You do not need eighteen. You need three.

::: key-idea The 10-Second Read
**1. How many servings am I actually eating?**
Every other number on the panel is per serving. Until this is settled, nothing else on the panel means what you think it means.

**2. What are the first three ingredients?**
They are listed heaviest first. The first three are most of the food.

**3. What's the one number I care about today?**
Not all of them. One. Fiber this week, sodium next week, added sugars the week after.
:::

That is the whole system. Three questions, in that order, on any package in any store.

The order is not decorative. Question 1 comes first because serving size is the multiplier on every number below it, including the ones in Chapter 6 that can legally be printed as zero. Question 2 comes second because the ingredient list is the recipe and the panel is only the summary. Question 3 comes last because it is the only one that requires you to decide anything, and deciding is easier once the first two are settled.

Ten seconds is not a marketing number. Once you know where to look, it is genuinely how long it takes.

### What this book is not

It is not a diet. Nobody in this book is on one. There are no targets in here for you or for your child, no tracking, no lists of things to remove from your kitchen, and no foods described as good or bad. That is a deliberate design decision, it is grounded in pediatric guidance, and Chapter 8 explains exactly why we made it.

You are being handed one skill. Nothing is being taken away.

# 1. Why Nobody Reads the Label

You have read a label before. Probably this week.

Here is what actually happened. You turned the box over. Your eyes landed somewhere in the middle of a grid of numbers. You registered the word "sugar," possibly a percentage, definitely nothing you could have repeated ten seconds later. Then you put the box in the cart, because the kid was asking and the freezer section was still ahead of you.

That is not a literacy failure. That is a design encounter with eighteen lines and no instructions.

The common story about label reading is that people read labels wrong. That is not what the evidence in a grocery store looks like. People do not misread labels. **They never look, because nobody ever told them which lines matter.** Handed eighteen data points and six seconds, a reasonable adult reads zero of them. That is the correct response to an unsorted list.

::: callout
The front of the box is an ad. The back of the box is a spec sheet.
:::

## Why the back of the box is worth your time

Here is the part that surprises most people: the manufacturer does not decide what goes on the Nutrition Facts panel, or in what order.

FDA does. The panel is required on virtually all packaged food made in or imported into the United States. The line order is fixed by regulation. Serving size sits where it sits, calories are set in that oversized type, and added sugars appear indented under total sugars, because a federal rule says so and not because a marketing team chose it.

The front of the package is the opposite. The front is where a company gets to talk. Everything on the front was chosen, positioned, and tested to sell you the thing.

So the two faces of a box are not two opinions about the same food. One is an argument. The other is a disclosure. Chapter 7 is entirely about what the argument is legally allowed to say, and you will enjoy it.

## The current panel is newer than you think

The label you are holding is the product of a 2016 final rule, the first major overhaul in more than twenty years. Three things changed that matter to a family:

- **Calories got big.** Large, bold type, because calorie information was the most-sought and most-missed item on the old label.
- **Added sugars appeared.** A whole new line, indented under total sugars, with its own percentage. Chapter 5 is about this one line.
- **The required vitamins changed.** Vitamin A and vitamin C stopped being mandatory. Vitamin D and potassium became mandatory, joining calcium and iron.

That last one is worth a second. The four micronutrients on your panel are not "the four most important nutrients." They are a snapshot of what the U.S. population is currently short on. Vitamin A and vitamin C deficiencies are rare here now; vitamin D and potassium are under-consumed. The list is a public-health readout, and it can change again.

::: parent-note
Nearly every line on the panel exists because someone had to justify it in a rulemaking. When a line looks strange, there is usually a reason, and the reason is usually more interesting than the conspiracy version.
:::

## What you are being handed

Three questions. One chapter each. Then five worked labels read end to end, and a chapter on doing this with a child without turning it into a thing.

You will not memorize anything. You do not need to know that the Daily Value for sodium is 2,300 mg, and you will not be quizzed on it. You need to know where to look, in what order, and what the number is a percentage of.

That is a ten-second skill. It survives being tired.

# 2. Step One: How Many Servings Am I Actually Eating?

The bag is open on the couch. It is a normal bag, roughly the size of a bag. Nobody measured anything.

Later, someone looks at the back and says: one hundred fifty calories, that's fine.

The bag says one hundred fifty calories per serving, and it holds about ten servings.

This is the single most under-taught line on the panel, and it is at the very top, in small type, above everything else: **servings per container.**

::: key-idea Everything below the top is per serving
Calories, sodium, added sugars, fiber, every percentage in the right-hand column. All of it is per serving. Until you know how many servings you are eating, no other number on the panel means what you think it means.
:::

## Serving size is not a recommendation

This is the most common misunderstanding about the panel, and it is worth being precise about.

Serving sizes are set from FDA reference amounts called RACCs, which stands for Reference Amounts Customarily Consumed. The key word is **customarily**. By law, serving sizes must be based on the amount of a food people **typically** consume in one sitting, not the amount anyone thinks they **should** consume.

Where did the amounts come from? National food consumption surveys. The originals used data from the late 1970s and late 1980s; the 2016 rule updated them with more recent survey data, which is why serving sizes for things like ice cream and soft drinks got **bigger**. FDA was not endorsing eating more. It was documenting what people actually do.

So a serving size is a measuring unit, chosen so you can compare two products fairly. It is not an instruction.

::: myth "The serving size is how much I'm supposed to eat."
It is a comparison unit. Federal rules require serving sizes to reflect what people typically eat, not what anyone should eat. The panel is telling you the scale of the measurement, not issuing a portion.
:::

## The multiplication moment

Here is the panel that teaches this best.

```nutrition-label
{
  "servingsPerContainer": "about 10",
  "servingSize": "about 15 chips (28g / 1 oz)",
  "calories": 150,
  "totalFat": 10, "saturatedFat": 1.5, "transFat": 0,
  "cholesterol": 0, "sodium": 170,
  "totalCarb": 15, "fiber": 1, "totalSugars": 0, "addedSugars": 0,
  "protein": 2,
  "vitaminD": 0, "calcium": 10, "iron": 0.4, "potassium": 350,
  "ingredients": "Potatoes, vegetable oil (sunflower, corn, and/or canola oil), salt.",
  "caption": "Representative example created for teaching. Not an actual product label."
}
```

Nothing on that panel is alarming per serving. That is the point.

Now do the arithmetic the panel does not do for you. Say a third of the bag goes, which is roughly three servings:

| Per serving (printed) | Three servings (not printed) |
|---|---|
| 150 calories | 450 calories |
| 10 g total fat | 30 g total fat |
| 1.5 g saturated fat | 4.5 g saturated fat |
| 170 mg sodium, 7% DV | 510 mg sodium, 22% DV |

Look at the sodium line. At one serving it sits at 7%, comfortably mid-range. At three servings it clears 20%, which is the threshold FDA's own consumer guidance calls high. Nothing changed about the food. The multiplier changed.

The panel will never print the right-hand column. You produce it. That is the job.

## The rule that made 20-ounce bottles honest

Some packages solve this for you, because a rule forces them to.

A product that is packaged and sold individually and contains **less than 200 percent** of its reference amount must be treated as a **single-serving container**. The entire contents get labeled as one serving. No arithmetic, no fractions.

This is why a 20-ounce bottle of soda now looks like this:

```nutrition-label
{
  "servingsPerContainer": "1",
  "servingSize": "1 bottle (20 fl oz / 591 mL)",
  "calories": 240,
  "totalFat": 0, "saturatedFat": 0, "transFat": 0,
  "cholesterol": 0, "sodium": 75,
  "totalCarb": 65, "fiber": 0, "totalSugars": 65, "addedSugars": 65,
  "protein": 0,
  "vitaminD": 0, "calcium": 0, "iron": 0, "potassium": 0,
  "ingredients": "Carbonated water, high fructose corn syrup, caramel color, phosphoric acid, natural flavors, caffeine.",
  "caption": "Representative example created for teaching. Not an actual product label."
}
```

Under the pre-2016 rules, that same bottle could be labeled as **2.5 servings**. Everything on the panel was a fraction of a bottle nobody drinks in fractions. Shoppers systematically under-read what they were drinking, and they were not being careless. They were reading a panel built around an amount that did not exist.

One rule change, and the whole bottle became the number. This is the best example in the store of a labeling rule changing what people actually see.

## The 2-to-3 servings rule

There is a middle case, and it has its own requirement.

If a package holds **at least 200 percent and up to 300 percent** of the reference amount, the law assumes a person might reasonably finish it in one sitting. So the label must carry **two columns**: one per serving, one per container.

| Package size, relative to the reference amount | What the label does |
|---|---|
| Under 150% | Single-serving container. The whole package is one serving. |
| More than 150%, less than 200% | Still a single-serving container. A second column at the reference amount is optional. |
| **At least 200% and up to 300%** | **Two columns required:** per serving and per container. |
| More than 300% | Ordinary per-serving labeling. |

When you see two columns, read the right one first. That is the honest number if the package gets finished.

Some categories are exempt, including foods that need further preparation and a few specific product types. If a package you expected to be dual-column is not, that is usually why.

::: heads-up
Serving size is also the multiplier on the rounding rules in Chapter 6. A smaller declared serving produces smaller printed numbers **and** more chances for a value to fall below a threshold and print as zero. This is why servings come first in the 10-Second Read and first in this book. Read Chapter 6 after this one, never before.
:::

::: aisle Mission: find the multiplier
Pick the one package your household reliably finishes in a single sitting. You know which one it is.

Find **servings per container**. Write it here: {{blank:short}}

Multiply the calories by that number. Then multiply the sodium by it. Say both out loud once.

You are not going to do anything about it. You are just going to know it. That is the mission.
:::

# 3. Step Two: What Are the First Three Ingredients?

The front of the box says MADE WITH WHOLE GRAIN in a font that means it.

Turn it over. First ingredient: enriched wheat flour.

Both of those statements are legal. Only one of them is load-bearing.

## Heaviest first

The ingredient list has one rule that does almost all the work: ingredients are listed in **descending order of predominance by weight.** Most of what is in the package, first. Least, last.

The weight is measured as each ingredient is added during manufacturing, not in the finished product after baking or moisture loss. That detail matters occasionally and confuses people constantly, so file it and move on.

The practical consequence is the skill: **the first three ingredients are most of the food.** Not a hint about the food. Most of it.

::: key-idea The list is the recipe. The panel is the summary.
The panel tells you how much of a nutrient. The ingredient list tells you what the thing is actually made of, in order, by weight.
:::

There are two exceptions worth knowing so you are not surprised.

Ingredients present at **2 percent or less by weight** do not have to be in descending order, as long as they sit at the end of the list after a statement like "Contains 2% or less of." And where a manufacturer uses a blend of fats or oils and varies the mixture, the items inside that parenthetical do not have to be in order either. That is why you see "vegetable oil (sunflower, corn, and/or canola oil)" on the chip bag in Chapter 2. The "and/or" is doing real legal work.

## The sweetener-splitting problem

Now the interesting part.

Because ingredients are ordered by weight, a single sweetener at the top of a list is conspicuous. Four different sweeteners, each individually smaller, are not, even when they add up to more than anything else in the package.

Sugar, corn syrup, honey, and fruit juice concentrate are four ingredients. They occupy four positions. None of them has to be first.

This is legal, it is common, and it is not a plot. It is just what sorting by weight does to a list when one category has many members. Here is a panel built to show it:

```nutrition-label
{
  "servingsPerContainer": "about 9",
  "servingSize": "1 cup (36g)",
  "calories": 140,
  "totalFat": 1, "saturatedFat": 0, "transFat": 0,
  "cholesterol": 0, "sodium": 160,
  "totalCarb": 32, "fiber": 1, "totalSugars": 13, "addedSugars": 12,
  "protein": 2,
  "vitaminD": 2, "calcium": 130, "iron": 4.5, "potassium": 60,
  "ingredients": "Corn flour blend (whole grain yellow corn flour, degerminated yellow corn flour), sugar, corn syrup, canola oil, salt, honey, natural flavor, annatto extract color. Vitamin E (mixed tocopherols) added to preserve freshness.",
  "caption": "Representative example created for teaching. Not an actual product label."
}
```

Three sweeteners in that list: sugar, corn syrup, honey. Spread across positions two, three, and six. Read the list alone and you would not confidently say how much sugar is in there.

Now read the panel. **Added sugars: 12 g.** Twenty-four percent of the Daily Value, in one cup.

That is the whole reason the Added Sugars line was created. **It re-aggregates what the ingredient list splits apart.** The list is the recipe, the line is the total, and you need both because neither one does the other's job.

::: callout
The ingredient list is the hunt. The Added Sugars line is the answer.
:::

## The names

There is no official FDA master list of sugar synonyms, and depending on who is counting there are sixty or more names in circulation. Do not memorize them. A family that tries will lose interest by Thursday.

Use the heuristic instead:

> **If it ends in -ose, or it contains the word syrup, nectar, malt, or cane, it is a sugar.**

That catches most of the list. Some examples of what you will actually see:

| Family | Names on the list |
|---|---|
| The obvious ones | sugar, brown sugar, raw sugar, powdered sugar, cane sugar |
| The -ose group | sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose, maltose |
| Corn-derived | corn syrup, corn syrup solids, high fructose corn syrup, crystalline fructose |
| Syrups | maple syrup, agave nectar, brown rice syrup, malt syrup, tapioca syrup |
| The wholesome-sounding ones | evaporated cane juice, turbinado, demerara, muscovado, sucanat, panela |
| Fruit-derived | fruit juice concentrate, grape juice concentrate, date sugar |
| Honey and molasses | honey, molasses, blackstrap molasses |

Two honest caveats, because the heuristic is a heuristic:

- **Maltodextrin** and **dextran** are sugars and break the -ose rule.
- **Lactose** in plain milk or plain yogurt is a naturally occurring sugar, not an added one. Chapter 5 shows exactly what that looks like on a panel.

And the important one: spotting a name is not a verdict. It is a fact about a list. The grams line is where the size of the thing lives.

::: try-it
Take one package out of your pantry. Read only the ingredient list. Circle every name that ends in -ose or contains syrup, nectar, malt, or cane.

How many did you find? {{blank:short}}

Now find the Added Sugars line on the same package and write the grams: {{blank:short}}

Those two answers are not the same kind of information. The first is what it is made of. The second is how much.
:::

## Whole grain, decoded

This one deserves its own section because the front of the box is loud about it and the rules are quiet.

**FDA has never issued a binding regulation defining "whole grain" for labeling.** It issued **draft** guidance in 2006. Draft guidance is not a rule, and no final rule has followed it.

So, plainly:

- **"Multigrain"** means several grains were used. It says nothing at all about whether any of them are whole.
- **"Made with whole grain"** means some whole grain is present. There is no required amount. It is frequently a minority of the grain in the package.
- The **Whole Grain Stamp** is a voluntary industry certification run by the Whole Grains Council, not an FDA program. It has its own criteria, and it is a private standard rather than a federal one.

What actually works is the same move you already learned: **read ingredient number one.**

If it says whole wheat flour, whole grain oats, or whole grain corn, then whole grain is the predominant grain by weight, and the front of the box is describing something real. If it says enriched wheat flour or just wheat flour, that is refined flour, no matter how large the banner is.

"Enriched" is not a synonym for whole. It means some of the nutrients removed in refining were partially added back.

::: heads-up Allergens, briefly
The United States requires declaration of **nine** major food allergens: milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, and **sesame**. Sesame became the ninth under the FASTER Act, with labeling required as of **January 1, 2023**, so older reference material and a lot of printed handouts still say eight.

Allergens appear either in the ingredient list itself or in a separate "Contains" statement next to it.

"May contain" and "processed in a facility with" statements are **voluntary and unregulated in their content.** They are not a graded risk scale and should not be read as one. If someone in your house has a diagnosed allergy, the package and your allergist are the authorities, not this book.
:::

::: aisle Mission: ingredient number one
Three boxes of the same kind of food. Cereal works, so does bread, so do crackers.

Read only the **first** ingredient on each. Nothing else. Do not turn to the panel.

Then, and only then, look at which box had the loudest front-of-package claim. Was it the same one?
:::

# 4. Step Three: What's the One Number I Care About Today?

Here is a better question than "is this good for me."

**What do you actually want to know about this box?**

Some weeks the answer is fiber, because breakfast is the same three things and you want more of it. Some weeks it is sodium, because soup season started. Some weeks it is added sugars. Once in a while it is protein, or calcium, or nothing at all.

Pick one. Read that line. Move on. That is step three, and it is deliberately not "read all of them," because reading all of them is how people end up reading none of them.

## What the percentage is a percentage of

The right-hand column is the **% Daily Value**, and almost nobody knows what it is measuring.

It is the percentage of a fixed reference amount, called the Daily Value, that one serving supplies. Those Daily Values are pegged to a **2,000-calorie-a-day reference diet.** The label says so, out loud, in the footnote at the bottom of every panel:

> The % Daily Value (DV) tells you how much a nutrient in a serving of food contributes to a daily diet. 2,000 calories a day is used for general nutrition advice.

Here are the values that column is computed from:

| Nutrient | Daily Value | Shown on the panel as |
|---|---|---|
| Total Fat | 78 g | grams and %DV |
| Saturated Fat | 20 g | grams and %DV |
| Cholesterol | 300 mg | mg and %DV |
| Sodium | 2,300 mg | mg and %DV |
| Total Carbohydrate | 275 g | grams and %DV |
| Dietary Fiber | 28 g | grams and %DV |
| Added Sugars | 50 g | grams and %DV |
| Protein | 50 g | grams; %DV usually blank |
| Vitamin D | 20 mcg | mcg and %DV |
| Calcium | 1,300 mg | mg and %DV |
| Iron | 18 mg | mg and %DV |
| Potassium | 4,700 mg | mg and %DV |

You do not need to memorize that table. It is here so that the percentages stop being mysterious.

## Your kid is not a 2,000-calorie day

This is the most important thing in the chapter, and it is why a parent-facing book on this subject has to exist at all.

The panel on the cereal box your seven-year-old is holding is calculated for **adults and children four years and older, on a 2,000-calorie reference diet.** There is no child-specific panel on ordinary groceries.

FDA does publish other Daily Value sets, four in total, including one for children one through three years and one for infants. Those only appear on food that is marketed to those groups. Everything else on the shelf uses the adult and 4-plus set.

So what does that mean in practice? A 2,000-calorie day is a large day for a small person. Every %DV on a package therefore **understates** the share of a young child's day that the food represents. A snack showing 20% of the Daily Value represents more than a fifth of what a small child's day can hold.

We are not going to print how much more, and that is on purpose. Calorie figures for individual children are outside what we can source cleanly, and handing a parent a child-specific calorie number is exactly the step that turns a reading skill into a tracking habit. Chapter 8 explains why we will not do that.

::: key-idea Comparison always works. Budgeting is approximate.
Use the %DV to compare two packages to each other. That is arithmetic against a fixed reference, and it is reliable for everybody at every age.

Use it as a personal daily budget gently, and never for a child.
:::

## The 5/20 rule, and the honest caveat

FDA publishes a rule of thumb on its consumer pages. This is its exact framing:

> As a general guide: **5% DV or less of a nutrient per serving is considered low**, and **20% DV or more of a nutrient per serving is considered high.**

That is a genuinely useful heuristic, and it is the single most portable thing in this chapter. It also cuts in both directions, which most people who quote it forget:

- For nutrients you are **limiting** — saturated fat, sodium, added sugars — you are generally looking at the low side.
- For nutrients you want **more of** — fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium — you are generally looking at the high side.

Now the caveat, which almost every article about this gets wrong.

**The 5/20 rule is FDA consumer education guidance, not a codified regulation.** It is FDA's own plain-language heuristic, published on its consumer-facing label pages. It is not a binding threshold in the labeling rules, and no manufacturer is compelled by it.

We tell you that for two reasons. First, because it is true, and this book cites its sources. Second, because it changes how you use it: 5/20 is a fast sorting tool, not a legal verdict. Say "FDA's general guide calls that high," not "that's illegally high."

Here is what those percentages look like in grams and milligrams, per serving:

| Nutrient | "Low" is 5% DV or less | "High" is 20% DV or more |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 115 mg or less | 460 mg or more |
| Saturated Fat | 1 g or less | 4 g or more |
| Added Sugars | 2.5 g or less | 10 g or more |
| Dietary Fiber | 1.4 g or less | 5.6 g or more |

Remember that 5.6 g fiber figure. It comes back in Chapter 7 and it is one of the best things in this book.

## Why some lines have no percentage at all

Three lines on the panel have a gram figure and no %DV. This is not an oversight, and each has a different reason.

| Line | Why there is no %DV | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Total Sugars** | FDA has not established a Daily Value for total sugars. | Read the indented **Added Sugars** line beneath it, which does have one. |
| **Trans Fat** | Expert bodies did not supply a reference value FDA considered sufficient to set a Daily Value. | Compare grams, and read the ingredient list. Chapter 6 explains why. |
| **Protein** | The %DV is **voluntary** for general-population foods. | Compare grams between products. |

Protein deserves the longer answer, because the blank on that line generates a myth.

A protein %DV is required only if the package makes a protein claim, or if the food is represented as being for children under four. Otherwise it is optional — and calculating it legally requires correcting for protein **quality** using a laboratory test called PDCAAS, which analyzes the food's essential amino acid profile and digestibility. That costs money. If you are not making a protein claim, there is no upside.

::: myth "A blank protein %DV means the food has no useful protein."
It means the manufacturer did not pay for the test. That is all it means. Compare the grams between two packages instead.
:::

# 5. Added Sugars

A single-serve yogurt cup, the flavored kind, the one that fits in a lunch bag.

Total Sugars: 19 g. Added Sugars: 11 g.

Most people, shown those two numbers, assume one of them is a typo. Neither is. The gap between them is the most useful thing on the panel, and the reason this line exists at all.

## The difference, exactly

**Total Sugars** is every sugar in the food. Naturally occurring ones included: lactose in milk, fructose in fruit, the sugars in vegetables.

**Added Sugars** is the subset that was put in. Specifically, it includes:

- sugars added during processing,
- foods packaged as sweeteners, like table sugar,
- sugars from syrups and honey,
- sugars from concentrated fruit or vegetable juices, above what the same volume of 100% juice of that type would naturally contain.

It does **not** include the naturally occurring sugars in milk, fruit, and vegetables.

Here is the yogurt:

```nutrition-label highlight
{
  "servingsPerContainer": "1",
  "servingSize": "1 container (150g)",
  "calories": 150,
  "totalFat": 2, "saturatedFat": 1.5, "transFat": 0,
  "cholesterol": 10, "sodium": 80,
  "totalCarb": 24, "fiber": 0, "totalSugars": 19, "addedSugars": 11,
  "protein": 6,
  "vitaminD": 0, "calcium": 190, "iron": 0, "potassium": 240,
  "ingredients": "Cultured grade A reduced fat milk, cane sugar, strawberries, corn starch, natural flavor, pectin.",
  "caption": "Representative example created for teaching. Not an actual product label. Shaded cells show FDA's low / moderate / high bands."
}
```

Nineteen grams of total sugar. Eleven grams added. **The 8-gram difference is lactose**, the sugar that is in milk before anyone does anything to it.

Before the 2016 rule, this panel would have shown you 19 g and stopped. You would have had no way to separate the milk from the cane sugar, and a plain yogurt and a flavored one would have looked closer together than they are.

::: key-idea Total Sugars says what is in there. Added Sugars says what was put in there.
Only one of them has a %DV. Read the indented line.
:::

## Grams to teaspoons

Grams are a bad unit for human beings. Nobody has ever pictured eleven grams of anything.

Teaspoons are a good unit, because you own a teaspoon.

> **Grams of added sugar, divided by 4, is roughly teaspoons.**

So the yogurt cup's 11 g of added sugars is {{tsp:11}}. Suddenly you can see it.

Here is the conversion in a form you can read off:

| Added sugars on the label | In teaspoons | %DV |
|---|---|---|
| 2 g | {{tsp:2}} | 4% |
| 4 g | {{tsp:4}} | 8% |
| 5 g | {{tsp:5}} | 10% |
| 10 g | {{tsp:10}} | 20% |
| 12 g | {{tsp:12}} | 24% |
| 20 g | {{tsp:20}} | 40% |
| 50 g | {{tsp:50}} | 100% |

::: heads-up An honest note about the division
A teaspoon of granulated sugar is actually about **4.2 grams**, not 4. Dividing by 4 slightly **over**states the teaspoon count.

We use 4 anyway, because it is the standard teaching conversion and because you can do it in your head in a grocery aisle, which is the only place this arithmetic is ever going to happen. The real teaspoon count is a touch lower than what you calculate. Now you know both.
:::

## Where 50 grams comes from

The Added Sugars Daily Value is **50 g**, and it is not arbitrary. It is 10% of a 2,000-calorie day: 10% of 2,000 is 200 calories, and sugar supplies 4 calories per gram, so 200 divided by 4 is 50 g.

Fifty grams is {{tsp:50}}. That is what 100% on the added sugars line means.

Apply the 5/20 rule to it and you get two thresholds worth carrying around:

- **2.5 g or less** of added sugars per serving is low.
- **10 g or more** per serving is high.

## Why the line exists

This matters for how you talk about it, so it is worth stating precisely.

FDA required the added sugars declaration because adding sugars to food increases calories without contributing other nutrients, which makes it harder to meet nutrient needs within a given number of calories.

That is a **nutrient-density** argument. It is not a morality argument. The line was not put there to mark food as wrong. It was put there because, before it existed, there was no way to see that information on a package at all.

Say it that way at your kitchen table and you will have said something true, useful, and completely un-loaded.

::: parent-note What about the new Dietary Guidelines?
The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans were released in January 2026, and they changed the shape of the added-sugar recommendation: away from a daily percentage-of-calories cap and toward a per-meal amount. They also extended the recommendation to avoid added sugars to older children than the previous edition did, and they state that no amount of added sugars is recommended as part of a nutritious diet.

Two things you should know about that.

**First, the exact figures and ages are being reported inconsistently across otherwise reliable sources right now.** We are not printing numbers we cannot stand behind, so we have described the change and left the figures out.

**Second, and this is the one that matters for reading a label: the guidelines did not change the label.** The Added Sugars Daily Value is still 50 g, and every %DV in the store is still computed from 50 g. Changing a Daily Value requires FDA rulemaking, and that has not happened. If you see a headline implying the panel now reflects the new guidelines, it does not.
:::

Pediatric and cardiology bodies publish their own added-sugar guidance for children, and it is lower than the label's 50 g reference. We are deliberately not printing a child's number in this book, because a child's number is a target, and targets for children are the one thing this product will not do. Chapter 8 is the long version of that decision.

# 6. The Rounding Rules Nobody Tells You About

A column of zeros is a strange thing to look at.

Zero grams of fat. Zero grams of trans fat. Zero grams of added sugars. Zero, zero, zero, all the way down one side of a panel, on a food that plainly contains things.

There is a rule behind every one of those zeros, and the rule is published. This chapter is about knowing it.

::: heads-up Read this before the rest of the chapter
The point of this chapter is **how the system works**, not that anyone is lying to you. Every number on a panel is rounded, the rounding increments are set out in federal regulation, and a company that follows them is complying with the law rather than evading it.

The conspiracy version of this chapter is more fun and less true. We are going to do the true one, because you can act on it.
:::

## Everything on a panel is rounded

Think about an odometer. It shows whole miles. It is not lying about the tenths; it just does not report them, and once you know that, you stop expecting it to.

The panel is the same. Every line has a required rounding increment, and below a certain amount the rule says to print zero.

| Line | How it rounds | Where it can legally print "0" |
|---|---|---|
| **Calories** | To the nearest 5 up to 50 calories; to the nearest 10 above that | Under 5 calories may be declared as 0 |
| **Total Fat** | To the nearest 0.5 g below 5 g; to the nearest 1 g at or above 5 g | Under 0.5 g prints as 0 g |
| **Saturated Fat** | Same as total fat | Under 0.5 g prints as 0 g |
| **Trans Fat** | Same as total fat | **Under 0.5 g must print as 0 g** |
| **Sodium** | To the nearest 5 mg from 5 to 140 mg; to the nearest 10 mg above 140 mg | Under 5 mg prints as 0 mg |
| **Total Carbohydrate, Fiber, Total Sugars, Added Sugars, Protein** | To the nearest 1 g | Under 0.5 g prints as 0 g |

You will also sometimes see "Contains less than 1 g" printed instead of a number. That is a permitted alternative on some lines, and it is a more honest-looking version of the same rounding.

::: key-idea A printed zero means "below the reporting threshold," not "none."
The threshold is published. Now you know where it is.
:::

## Three consequences worth carrying

**One. "0 g trans fat" can mean up to 0.49 g per serving.** This is the famous one. If a serving contains less than half a gram of trans fat, the rule requires it to be declared as 0 g. Not permits. Requires.

**Two. "0 calories" can mean up to 4.9 calories per serving.** On its own, irrelevant. Combined with a small serving size and a container holding many servings, small numbers compound quietly.

**Three. Serving size is the multiplier on every rounding rule.** A smaller declared serving produces smaller numbers and more opportunities for a value to drop below a rounding floor. This is why Chapter 2 comes before this chapter. The two mechanisms are the same mechanism.

## The trans fat story, told honestly

Here is where most consumer coverage stops, and where this book keeps going, because stopping there leaves you with a scarier and less accurate picture than the facts support.

In **2015**, FDA determined that partially hydrogenated oils — the primary source of artificial trans fat in the food supply — are **not Generally Recognized as Safe.** Most uses had a compliance cutoff of June 18, 2018, extended for certain remaining uses to **January 1, 2021**, with administrative cleanup completed in 2023.

Meaning: in 2026, industrially produced trans fat in U.S. packaged food is far less common than the "loophole" framing suggests. The loophole is real. The thing it used to conceal has largely been removed from the supply.

Trans fat has not vanished entirely. It occurs naturally in small amounts in meat and dairy from ruminant animals, and at very low levels in some edible oils. FDA's action on partially hydrogenated oils did not affect those natural sources.

And here is the verification move, which takes two seconds and settles it:

> **If the panel says 0 g trans fat, scan the ingredient list for the words "partially hydrogenated." If that phrase is not there, the zero is almost certainly a true zero.**

That is the whole check. One phrase, one list.

## Why the numbers do not add up

At some point you will multiply out the grams of fat, carbohydrate, and protein by their calorie values, compare the total to the printed Calories, and find they do not match.

They are not supposed to. **Each line rounds independently.** Ten rounded numbers do not reconcile to an eleventh rounded number, and no regulation requires them to.

This is normal and legal. It is also the single most common piece of evidence offered in the "labels are fake" genre of internet video, and now you know why it is not evidence of anything.

::: try-it
Find a package in your kitchen with a **0 g trans fat** line. It will not take long.

Now read its ingredient list all the way through, looking for the words "partially hydrogenated."

Did you find them? **yes / no**

If no, that zero is a real zero and you have just verified a label yourself, in about ten seconds, using a rule almost nobody knows. If yes, you have found something genuinely uncommon in 2026 and you should look at the serving size next.
:::

# 7. What the Front of the Box Is Allowed to Say

Two boxes. Same food. Same shelf.

One of them is quiet. The other one says GOOD SOURCE OF FIBER and EXCELLENT SOURCE OF IRON and MADE WITH WHOLE GRAIN and LIGHTLY SWEETENED.

Here is the thing that makes this chapter useful: **two of those four claims have exact legal definitions with numbers attached, and two have none at all.** Knowing which is which is the entire skill.

## The claims that are defined

These have thresholds in the regulations. A package that uses them has to meet them.

| Claim | What it legally requires |
|---|---|
| **Good source of / Contains / Provides** | 10% to 19% of the Daily Value per reference amount |
| **High in / Rich in / Excellent source of** | 20% or more of the Daily Value per reference amount |
| **Fat free** | Less than 0.5 g of total fat |
| **Low fat** | 3 g or less of total fat |
| **Low saturated fat** | 1 g or less of saturated fat, and no more than 15% of calories from saturated fat |
| **Very low sodium** | 35 mg or less of sodium |
| **Low sodium** | 140 mg or less of sodium |
| **Sugar free** | Less than 0.5 g of sugars, with no ingredient that is a sugar or is generally understood to contain sugars |
| **Reduced / Less / Fewer** | At least 25% less of that nutrient than a reference food, and the reference food and the percentage difference must be stated next to the claim |
| **Light / Lite** | If the food gets 50% or more of its calories from fat: fat reduced by at least half. If less than 50% of calories from fat: either calories cut by at least a third, or fat cut by at least half. "Light in sodium" means sodium reduced by at least half. |

Two notes on reading that table.

The thresholds are assessed per **reference amount** as well as per labeled serving. This is the RACC from Chapter 2 doing more work.

And **"light" is a comparison, not a category.** Light compared to what? The package has to tell you. If you find yourself unable to work out what a "reduced" or "light" product is being compared to, look harder, because the comparison is legally required to be printed adjacent to the claim.

**"No sugar added"** deserves its own paragraph. It means no sugars or sugar-containing ingredients were added during processing or packing, and that the product contains no ingredients whose function is to add sweetness. It does **not** mean the food contains no sugar, because naturally occurring sugars are untouched by the claim. And if the food is not low-calorie or calorie-reduced, the label is required to say so near the claim.

## The claim thresholds moved, and most articles never noticed

This is the best thing in this chapter and almost nobody prints it correctly.

"Good source" and "high" are defined as **percentages of the Daily Value.** They are not defined in grams.

So when a Daily Value changes, every gram cutoff attached to it changes too, silently, with no announcement and no press release.

Fiber is the clearest case. The Daily Value for fiber was **25 g** before the 2016 rule. It is **28 g** now.

| Claim | Under the old 25 g DV | Under the current 28 g DV |
|---|---|---|
| "Good source of fiber" (10% of DV, entry point) | 2.5 g | **2.8 g** |
| "High fiber" / "Excellent source of fiber" (20% of DV) | 5 g | **5.6 g** |

A very large number of consumer articles, nutrition handouts, and well-meaning blog posts still print 2.5 g and 5 g. Those are retired figures. They were correct before 2016 and they are wrong now.

::: key-idea Percentages are the definition. Grams are just today's translation.
Any time you see a gram threshold quoted for a "good source" or "high in" claim, check when it was written. If the Daily Value has moved since, the gram figure has moved with it.
:::

## The claims that are not defined at all

Now the other half of the shelf.

| Term | Legal status |
|---|---|
| **"Natural"** | **FDA has never made a rule defining it.** It operates a longstanding informal policy: nothing artificial or synthetic that you would not expect to be in the food. That policy was never intended to address production methods such as pesticide use, and does not address processing methods such as pasteurization or irradiation. FDA asked for public comment. As of 2026 there is still no binding definition. |
| **"Lightly sweetened," "less sweet," "just a little sweet"** | **No legal definition.** FDA has not defined "low sugar" or "high sugar" either. Products carrying terms like these have been documented containing 14 to 25 grams of added sugar per serving, up to half the 50 g Daily Value. |
| **"Multigrain"** | No definition tied to whole grain content. Several grains, whole or not. |
| **"Made with whole grain"** | No binding quantity threshold. Draft guidance only. |
| **"Zero trans fat"** | Not a defined claim at all. It is a restatement of a panel value produced by the rounding rule in Chapter 6. |

::: myth "'Natural' is a regulated term."
It is a policy, not a rule. FDA has never conducted rulemaking to define "natural" on a food label, and the informal policy it does apply says nothing about how the food was grown or how it was processed. Treat the word as decoration.
:::

## Organic is the exception, and it is not FDA's

"Organic" is genuinely defined, precisely, with teeth. It is just not a nutrition claim and it is not FDA's program. It is USDA's.

| What the label says | What it requires | USDA Organic seal allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| **100 Percent Organic** | 100% certified organic ingredients | Yes |
| **Organic** | At least 95% certified organic content | Yes |
| **Made with organic ___** | At least 70% certified organic content | **No** |
| Below 70% organic | Organic ingredients may be identified only within the ingredient list | No |

Organic is a **production-method** certification. It describes how something was grown and handled. It says nothing at all about added sugars, sodium, or calories.

An organic cookie is a cookie.

## "Healthy" is mid-transition right now

This one is genuinely in motion, and if you have noticed the word appearing on things it did not used to appear on, you are not imagining it.

"Healthy" is a regulated nutrient content claim with a defined meaning. FDA published an **updated** definition as a final rule on **December 27, 2024.** Its effective date was delayed to **April 28, 2025.** The **compliance date is February 25, 2028.**

What that means in practice, right now, in a store:

- Manufacturers **may** use the new criteria today.
- They are **not required** to until February 25, 2028.
- So between now and then, the word "healthy" on a package may reflect **either** standard, and there is no way to tell which from the outside.

The new criteria require a food to contain a meaningful amount of an actual food group — fruit, vegetables, whole grains, dairy, or protein foods — and to stay under limits on added sugars, sodium, and saturated fat that vary by category. The old limits on total fat and cholesterol were removed. The added sugars limit is new.

The reshuffle is real: foods that previously could **not** carry the claim, such as nuts, seeds, higher-fat fish like salmon, certain oils, and water, now can. Foods that previously could carry it will **lose** it by February 2028, including fortified white bread, highly sweetened cereal, fortified fruit punch that is not 100% juice, and high-added-sugar fruit snacks.

Practical position, and it is also the safest one: **ignore the word and read the panel.** For the next couple of years it carries two possible meanings, which is the same as carrying none.

## What is coming: the box on the front

FDA has **proposed** putting a standardized nutrition summary on the **front** of most packaged foods. A "Nutrition Info" box, in the top third of the front panel, showing serving size plus saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars, each with its %DV and an interpretive rating.

The proposed ratings are **Low at 5% DV or less, Med at 6% to 19%, and High at 20% or more.**

Read those cutoffs again. That is the 5/20 rule from Chapter 4, made mandatory and moved to the front of the box.

**Status, stated plainly: proposed, not required.** The rule was proposed January 16, 2025. The comment period closed July 15, 2025. As of this writing, **no final rule has been issued.** It remains on FDA's priority list, and the proposal allows about three years for larger firms and four for smaller ones after any final rule. None of that clock has started.

Which gives the skill in this book an unusually long shelf life. You are learning the interpretation system the future label is likely to print for you for free.

# 8. How to Teach This to Your Kid Without Messing Them Up

Your seven-year-old hands a box back to you in the aisle and says, in your exact voice, "we shouldn't get that one, it's bad."

You never said that. Not in those words. But something got through, and what got through was not a reading skill. It was a verdict.

This chapter is the most important one in this book, and it is the reason a label-literacy product aimed at families needs a parent guide at all. Teaching a child to read a panel is easy. Teaching a child to read a panel without teaching them to police themselves takes a little care.

## What the evidence actually says

This is not a vibe. It is one of the better-studied areas in pediatric feeding, and the findings are consistent enough to plan around.

**Restricting a food increases a child's interest in it.** Restricting access focuses a child's attention on the restricted food and increases the desire to obtain and eat it. When the restriction lifts, intake goes up. Researchers have called this the forbidden-fruit effect for a long time, and it is remarkably reliable.

**Restriction interferes with self-regulation.** In Birch and Fisher's work with seven-year-old girls, girls whose eating was more restricted at home not only ate more of those snack foods when given access, they reported **negative feelings about their own eating.** Restrictive feeding practices are associated with impaired ability to regulate intake, and high restriction at age five predicted eating in the absence of hunger in the years that followed.

**Dieting is a risk factor, not a tool.** The American Academy of Pediatrics is direct about this in its clinical report on prevention: dieting is a shared risk factor for the outcomes families most want to avoid, on both ends.

AAP's recommendations to families, summarized:

| AAP guidance | In practice |
|---|---|
| **Do not talk about weight.** Talk about eating and being active. | Bodies are not a topic. Not theirs, not yours, not anyone's at the table. |
| **Discourage dieting and meal skipping.** | Support eating patterns that can be maintained indefinitely. |
| **Focus on habits, not weight.** | The habit here is *reading*. That is all we are installing. |
| **Promote positive body image.** | Never use body dissatisfaction as motivation for anything. |
| **Encourage family meals.** | More of them, when you can manage it. |

AAP-affiliated guidance for families adds four specifics that map directly onto this book: avoid labeling foods good or bad in front of a child; avoid commenting on their body or anyone else's; avoid using food as a reward or a punishment; and teach children to use hunger and fullness cues rather than external triggers like how much is on the plate.

And one reassurance worth holding onto, because parents reading this chapter tend to overcorrect into paralysis: AAP notes that efforts to support children's health, **conducted correctly**, do not predispose children to eating disorders. Doing this well is possible. It is mostly a matter of what you say out loud.

::: callout
This is a reading product, not a restriction product. The goal is that your family can find and compare the numbers on a package. Nothing here tells anyone what to eat, how much, or what their body should look like.
:::

## The scripts

Actual sentences. Steal them. Say them in your own voice, badly, at the wrong moment. It still works.

### When they ask "is this bad for me?"

> "Foods aren't good or bad. This one has more sugar in it than that one. Want to see where it says so?"

You have answered the question, refused the frame, and turned it into a reading task, in three sentences.

### When they want the box with the cartoon on it

> "Sure. Let's buy it and read it together."

Buying it is not a defeat. **Reading it is the lesson.** A child who is allowed to pick the loud box and then gets to find the added sugars line on it learns more than a child who is told no and learns only that the loud box is forbidden, which is precisely the condition the research says increases its appeal.

### When the answer is genuinely no

Say no for the actual reason. Almost never is the actual reason nutritional.

> "Not today. Not because of what's in it. We're not buying a fourth box of cereal."

> "That one's eight dollars. Pick a different one."

The reason matters more than the answer. "No, that food is bad" teaches a rule about food. "No, we already have three" teaches a rule about groceries.

### When they call a food bad

> "Where'd you hear that? Foods don't have a personality. Let's look at what's actually in it."

Curious, not corrective. They probably heard it from a video, a friend, or an adult who meant well.

### When another adult moralizes at the table

You are not going to win an argument with a relative over dinner, and you should not try in front of the kid.

> "We're doing a reading thing right now, not a rules thing."

Then change the subject. The kid hears the frame, which is all you needed.

### When they ask how much of something they should eat

Hand it back to them, gently.

> "Your body's better at that than I am. Are you still hungry?"

That is hunger-and-fullness language, it is what AAP guidance actually recommends, and it keeps you out of the business of issuing portions.

### When they catch you being inconsistent

> "You're right. Good catch."

Let them be right. A kid who catches an adult in an inconsistency and gets credit for it will read labels for years. A kid who gets corrected for it will stop looking.

### When they get a number wrong

> "Wrong and then checking is the whole job."

### When you are tired and do not want to do this today

Then do not. The skill keeps. There is no streak to maintain and nothing is undone by a week off.

## What to say instead

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "That's junk." | Name the food. "Those are the cheese crackers." |
| "This one's healthy." | "This one has more fiber. Here's the line." |
| "That has so much sugar in it." | "That's 12 grams of added sugar. Want to know what that is in teaspoons?" |
| "You've had enough." | "Are you still hungry?" |
| "If you eat your dinner you can have dessert." | Serve them together, or do not connect them at all. |
| "I'm being so bad today." | Nothing. Say nothing. They are listening to this one hardest. |

That last row is the one that changes the most in a household. Children learn food language from adults talking about themselves, not from adults talking to them.

## What the two age groups can actually do

Children are not small adults, and the difference is not motivation. It is cognitive stage.

| | **Roughly ages 6 to 9** | **Roughly ages 10 to 13** |
|---|---|---|
| **What they can genuinely do** | Count servings. Find a named line. Compare two numbers. See a pile of teaspoons and understand it. | Work with percentages. Compare two packages on a chosen nutrient. Understand that a statement can be technically true and still misleading. |
| **What is still out of reach** | A percentage of an unseen daily total. "2,000 calories" as an abstraction. Why a company would word something carefully. | Long-horizon consequences still feel abstract, which is normal and not a character flaw. |
| **What works** | Hands-on hunting. "Find the Added Sugars line." "How many servings in this box?" | Side-by-side comparisons. Claim-spotting. "Which one gives more fiber per serving, and how do you know?" |
| **What to avoid entirely** | Any personal target of any kind. | Any personal target of any kind, and any reference to their body. |

The short version: **the younger group finds and counts. The older group compares and questions. Neither group gets a number that is about them.**

::: heads-up The stopping rule
If reading labels starts to make mealtimes tense, if your child starts refusing foods they used to eat, if they begin counting or tracking anything about themselves, or if this becomes a source of anxiety for anyone in the house — stop.

Put the book on the shelf. Talk to your pediatrician. The skill will keep, and nothing in this book is worth more than a kid who is relaxed at dinner.
:::

## 1. Plain toasted oat cereal

```nutrition-label
{
  "servingsPerContainer": "about 8",
  "servingSize": "1 1/2 cups (39g)",
  "calories": 140,
  "totalFat": 2.5, "saturatedFat": 0.5, "transFat": 0,
  "cholesterol": 0, "sodium": 190,
  "totalCarb": 29, "fiber": 4, "totalSugars": 2, "addedSugars": 2,
  "protein": 5,
  "vitaminD": 2, "calcium": 130, "iron": 8, "potassium": 250,
  "ingredients": "Whole grain oats, corn starch, sugar, salt, tripotassium phosphate. Vitamin E (mixed tocopherols) added to preserve freshness.",
```

*About eight servings, and the serving is a cup and a half. That is a realistic bowl, so I do not have to multiply anything. Good.*

*First ingredient is whole grain oats. That is the whole-grain question answered without reading the front of the box. Sugar is third, and the added sugars line says 2 grams, which is {{tsp:2}} — so third place here is not doing much.*

*Today I care about fiber. Four grams, 14% of the Daily Value. That is mid-range, not high — high would be 5.6 grams. The line I did not expect is sodium at 8%. Cereal is a quiet sodium source and I never think about it.*

**The read:** low in added sugars, strong on iron, fiber is respectable but not remarkable. Sodium is the line worth noticing.

# 10. Take It to the Store

Tuesday, 5:40 p.m. One kid in the cart, one kid walking and negotiating. You have eleven items on the list and you would like to be home.

This is the environment. Any version of this that requires calm does not survive contact with a Tuesday.

So here are the rules of the game, and they are designed for Tuesday.

## Four rules that keep it a game

**One. It is three minutes, not a lesson.** One mission per trip. Sometimes one mission per aisle, if it is going well. Never a tour of the whole store.

**Two. Nothing changes because of it.** The shopping list is not up for renegotiation based on what a seven-year-old finds on a panel. Say that out loud at the start. It removes every incentive to perform an answer and it is the difference between a game and an interrogation.

**Three. They get to be right.** Let them find it. Let them tell you. Do not finish their sentence, and do not correct the small stuff.

**Four. Stop before they are done, not after.** End it while they still want another one. This is the single highest-leverage rule on this page.

::: key-idea Ten seconds once a week beats an hour once.
Nobody sustains a curriculum in a grocery store. Everybody can sustain one question in one aisle.
:::

## The missions

Pick one. Not four.

::: aisle Mission 1 — The servings hunt
"Find the package on this shelf with the most servings per container."

*Ages 6 to 9.* Pure searching and number comparison, no interpretation. It is the best first mission there is.
:::

::: aisle Mission 2 — Two boxes, same food
Hand them two packages of the same kind of thing.

"Which one gives you more fiber per serving? How do you know?"

*Ages 10 to 13.* The "how do you know" is the whole mission. Make them point at the line.
:::

::: aisle Mission 3 — Front to back
"Find something the front of this box says. Now find the line on the back that it's talking about."

*Either age.* Sometimes the line is there and the claim checks out. Sometimes there is no line, because the claim is one of the undefined ones. Both endings are a win.
:::

::: aisle Mission 4 — Ingredient number one
Three packages of the same food. Read only the first ingredient on each. Nothing else.

*Ages 6 to 9.* Fast, concrete, and it teaches the whole descending-by-weight idea without ever explaining it.
:::

::: aisle Mission 5 — The zero hunt
"Find a zero on a panel. Now figure out which rule made it a zero."

*Ages 10 to 13 only.* This one needs Chapter 6 and it needs abstraction. It is also the mission that makes a twelve-year-old feel genuinely clever, which is worth a lot.
:::

::: aisle Mission 6 — Teaspoon translation
Three packages. Added sugars, divided by four, out loud.

*Either age, and the best one for a car ride home.* Teaspoons are concrete in a way grams will never be.
:::

## Running it with a 7-year-old vs. a 12-year-old

| | **The 7-year-old** | **The 12-year-old** |
|---|---|---|
| Ask them to | **Find** and **count** | **Compare** and **question** |
| Good question | "How many servings are in this box?" | "Two boxes, same food. Which has more fiber per serving, and how do you know?" |
| The hook | Being the one who finds it first | Catching a package saying something technically true |
| Keep away from | Percentages, the 2,000-calorie idea, anything about themselves | Anything about themselves |
| How long | 90 seconds | As long as they are enjoying being right |

## When they ask for something

They will. That is not the mission failing, that is Tuesday. The move is the one from Chapter 8, repeated here because this is the aisle where you will need it:

> "Sure. Let's buy it and read it together."

If the answer has to be no, say no for the real reason. Budget, we already have three, we are out of time. Not the food's fault, because the food does not have a character.

## Build one

The best activity in this whole system is not reading a label. It is making one.

Give them a blank panel and let them invent a product. What is in it? How many servings? What does the front of the box say, and can they get it past you?

A kid who has written a front-of-package claim designed to survive their own parent has understood front-of-package claims permanently.

```nutrition-label blank
{
  "caption": "Blank panel in the standard format. Fill it in yourself. Representative format only."
}
```

::: activity The 30-day version
One package a day, for a month. No worksheets, no writing, no tracking, nothing to keep.

Just this, out loud, once a day, to whoever is in the room:

**"How many servings? What's first on the ingredient list? What's the one number today?"**

Thirty days of that and the ten-second read becomes automatic for everyone in the house, including you. Then you stop, because it is done, and you do not need a system for a thing you can already do.
:::

# The 10-Second Read

::: keep

**Step 1 — How many servings am I actually eating?**
Top of the panel. Servings per container, then serving size. Every number below this line is *per serving*. If two columns are printed, the right one is the whole package.

**Step 2 — What are the first three ingredients?**
Listed heaviest first. The first three are most of the food. Watch for sweeteners split across several names; the Added Sugars line re-totals them for you.

**Step 3 — What's the one number I care about today?**
One. Not all of them. Read that line's %DV.

---

### The numbers worth remembering

**FDA's general guide:** 5% DV or less is **low**. 20% DV or more is **high**.

| | Low is | High is |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 115 mg or less | 460 mg or more |
| Saturated fat | 1 g or less | 4 g or more |
| Added sugars | 2.5 g or less | 10 g or more |
| Fiber | 1.4 g or less | 5.6 g or more |

**Added sugars, grams to teaspoons:** divide by 4. The Daily Value is 50 g, which is {{tsp:50}}.

**A printed 0 g means "under the reporting threshold," not "none."** For trans fat that threshold is 0.5 g per serving. Check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated."

**Claim thresholds:** "good source" is 10–19% of the Daily Value. "High" and "excellent source" are 20% or more. Both are percentages, so their gram equivalents move when a Daily Value moves.

**Not defined by FDA at all:** natural · lightly sweetened · multigrain · made with whole grain.

:::

# Myth vs. Fact

| The myth | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "The serving size is how much I'm supposed to eat." | Serving sizes must be based on what people **typically** eat, not what anyone should eat. It is a comparison unit. |
| "0 g trans fat means there is none." | Anything under 0.5 g per serving must be declared as 0 g. Check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated." |
| "The %DV is calculated for my kid." | Every %DV on an ordinary grocery package is calculated for adults and children 4 and older, on a 2,000-calorie reference diet. There is no child panel on ordinary groceries. |
| "Total Sugars tells me how much sugar was added." | Total Sugars includes naturally occurring sugars from milk, fruit, and vegetables. The indented **Added Sugars** line is the one that isolates what was put in. |
| "A blank protein %DV means the food has no useful protein." | Protein %DV is voluntary unless a protein claim is made. Calculating it requires a lab test most manufacturers skip. Blank means no test, not no protein. |
| "'Natural' is regulated." | FDA has never made a rule defining it. Only an informal policy exists, and it does not address how food is grown or processed. |
| "'Lightly sweetened' means low in sugar." | No legal definition exists. Neither does one for "low sugar" or "high sugar." Products using such terms have carried 14 to 25 g of added sugar per serving. |
| "'Multigrain' and 'made with whole grain' mean whole grain." | "Multigrain" means several grains, whole or not. "Made with whole grain" has no required amount. Read the first ingredient. |
| "There are 8 major food allergens in the US." | There are **nine**. Sesame became the ninth, with labeling required as of January 1, 2023. |
| "If sugar isn't near the top of the ingredient list, there isn't much." | Sweeteners can be split across several names, so none has to reach the top. That is legal, and it is exactly why the Added Sugars line was created. |
| "If a package says 'healthy,' FDA has verified it is good for you." | The claim is mid-transition. The updated rule is not mandatory until February 25, 2028, so a package today may be using either standard. |
| "Front-of-package Low/Med/High boxes are already required." | They are **proposed**, not required. The proposal published January 16, 2025; comments closed July 15, 2025; no final rule has issued. |

:::

# What to Do Next

You are done. Genuinely — there is no level two of this.

Here is what actually makes it stick, in the order that works.

**This week: one package.** Not a system, not a plan. One package, one time, out loud, in your kitchen. Three questions. The first time you do it in front of someone else is the moment it becomes a household thing instead of a book thing.

**Next week: let a kid do it.** Pick the mission that matches their age from Chapter 10 and stop after three minutes. If they ask to do another one, do one more and then stop anyway.

**Then: pick your one number.** Not all of them, forever. One nutrient, for a season, because it is the one you are actually curious about. Change it whenever you want. Nobody is grading this.

**And when a package surprises you, that is the whole thing working.** Most will not. Most will be exactly what you expected, which means you were already reading them at some level. The occasional surprise is what the ten seconds bought you.
