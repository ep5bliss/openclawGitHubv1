# A. ANATOMY OF THE CURRENT NUTRITION FACTS PANEL

The Nutrition Facts label is required on virtually all packaged foods made in or imported into the US, and the FDA — not the manufacturer — decides what goes on it and in what order [S1][S28]. The current design is the product of a 2016 final rule, the first major overhaul in more than 20 years, driven by updated science and public comment [S4].

The panel reads top to bottom in a legally fixed order [S5][S50]. Here is every line, what it means, and why it is there.

| # | Line on the panel | What it actually tells you | Why it is on the label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Servings per container** | How many servings the manufacturer says are in the whole package. | Because every other number on the panel is *per serving*, not per package. If you miss this line you can misread the entire panel by a factor of 2, 3, or 8 [S1]. |
| 2 | **Serving size** (household measure + metric weight) | The amount of the food that all the numbers below refer to. Standardized by FDA so you can compare two products side by side. | It is standardized precisely so comparison shopping works. It is **not** a recommendation of how much to eat [S6][S41]. See Section C. |
| 3 | **Calories** | Energy in one serving. Printed in large, bold type since the 2016 redesign. | Made visually dominant because calorie information was the most-sought and most-missed item on the old label [S4][S28][S53]. |
| 4 | **Total Fat** (g + %DV) | All fat in a serving — saturated, trans, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated combined. | Retained as a total, but the 2016 rule moved emphasis away from total fat toward *type* of fat [S4]. |
| 5 | **Saturated Fat** (g + %DV) | The fat type associated with raised LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk. | One of the three "nutrients to limit" FDA highlights for consumers [S1]. |
| 6 | **Trans Fat** (g, **no %DV**) | Industrially produced and naturally occurring trans fatty acids. | Listed since 2006. No %DV exists because expert bodies did not supply a reference value FDA considered sufficient to set one [S52]. See Section H for the rounding trap. |
| 7 | **Cholesterol** (mg + %DV) | Dietary cholesterol. | Retained from the original label. The 2016 "healthy" reforms later de-emphasized it; the updated "healthy" claim rule removed cholesterol as a limiting criterion entirely [S18]. |
| 8 | **Sodium** (mg + %DV) | Sodium from salt and from every sodium-containing additive. | One of the three "nutrients to limit" [S1]. About 9 in 10 US children exceed sodium recommendations [S29]. |
| 9 | **Total Carbohydrate** (g + %DV) | Starch + fiber + sugars + sugar alcohols. The umbrella number. | The parent line for the three indented lines below it. |
| 10 | **Dietary Fiber** (g + %DV) | Fiber that meets FDA's post-2016 definition (intrinsic and intact fibers in plants, plus isolated/synthetic fibers FDA has accepted as having a physiological benefit). | Under-consumed nutrient; kept with a %DV to encourage intake [S1]. |
| 11 | **Total Sugars** (g, **no %DV**) | All sugars — naturally occurring (milk, fruit) plus added. | No %DV because FDA has not established a Daily Value for total sugars; compare grams instead [S1]. |
| 12 | **Added Sugars** — "Includes Xg Added Sugars" (g + %DV) | Sugars added during processing or packaging, plus syrups, honey, and sugars from concentrated fruit/vegetable juices. | **New in the 2016 rule.** This is the single most useful new line for a family. See Section D [S8]. |
| 13 | **Protein** (g, **%DV usually blank**) | Protein per serving. | %DV is voluntary for general-population foods; see Section E for exactly why it is almost always blank [S5]. |
| 14 | **Vitamin D** (mcg + %DV) | **Mandatory.** | Added in 2016 because it is a shortfall nutrient in the US population and matters for bone health [S4]. |
| 15 | **Calcium** (mg + %DV) | **Mandatory.** | Carried over; shortfall nutrient [S4][S5]. |
| 16 | **Iron** (mg + %DV) | **Mandatory.** | Carried over; shortfall nutrient [S4][S5]. |
| 17 | **Potassium** (mg + %DV) | **Mandatory.** | Added in 2016; relevant to blood pressure, and under-consumed [S4]. |
| 18 | **Footnote** | The %DV explainer. | Exact required wording below. |

### The four mandatory micronutrients — and the two that were dropped **[HIGH]**
- **Now mandatory (4):** vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium [S4][S5].
- **Dropped from mandatory status (2):** **vitamin A** and **vitamin C**. They may still be declared voluntarily, and must be declared if a claim is made about them or if they are added to the food [S4].
- **Why:** FDA updated the list to reflect current public-health nutrition. Vitamin A and vitamin C deficiencies are rare in the US today, whereas vitamin D and potassium are under-consumed by a meaningful share of the population [S4].
- **Teaching point for the parent track:** the mandatory micronutrient list is a snapshot of *what the US population is currently short on* — not a list of "the four most important nutrients." That reframing is honest and it is the kind of nuance that makes a paid product feel expensive.

### The footnote — exact required wording **[HIGH]**
> \*The % Daily Value (DV) tells you how much a nutrient in a serving of food contributes to a daily diet. 2,000 calories a day is used for general nutrition advice. [S5][S4]

Source note: some FDA consumer materials render this without the parenthetical "(DV)". Reproduce it with the parenthetical; that matches the regulatory text at 21 CFR 101.9(d)(9) [S5]. **[MEDIUM]** on the parenthetical specifically.

# B. THE %DAILY VALUE SYSTEM — WHERE EVERYONE GETS IT WRONG

## B1. What the %DV is actually based on **[HIGH]**
The %DV is the percentage of a **fixed reference Daily Value** that one serving supplies. Those Daily Values are pegged to a **2,000-calorie-a-day reference diet** [S1][S2][S5]. The label says this out loud in the footnote and then almost nobody reads it.

**Why this matters for a 7-year-old vs. a 40-year-old:**

| | Typical daily calorie need | Is 2,000 kcal a fit? | What the %DV does to them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sedentary 7-year-old | roughly 1,200–1,400 kcal | No — far above their need | Every %DV on the package **understates** the share of that child's day the food represents. A snack showing 20% DV added sugars may be closer to 30% of what a 7-year-old's smaller day can absorb. |
| Active 12-year-old | roughly 1,800–2,200 kcal | Roughly in range | %DV is a reasonable rough guide. |
| Sedentary 40-year-old woman | roughly 1,600–1,800 kcal | Somewhat above her need | %DV modestly understates. |
| Active 40-year-old man | roughly 2,400–2,800 kcal | Below his need | %DV modestly **overstates**. |

**The product line for parents (safe, accurate, non-moralizing):** *"The %DV is calculated for a 2,000-calorie day. Your kid is not a 2,000-calorie day. So use %DV to compare two packages to each other — that always works — and be gentle about treating it as a personal budget."* Calorie-need ranges above are generic illustrations and are tagged **[GAP]** as specific numbers; do not print age-specific calorie targets in the product (see Section I on why we deliberately avoid calorie targets for children).

## B2. The FDA's own "5/20 rule" — exact wording and where it is published **[HIGH]**
FDA's wording, on its consumer-facing label pages:

> "As a general guide: **5% DV or less of a nutrient per serving is considered low**, and **20% DV or more of a nutrient per serving is considered high**." [S1][S3]

**Where it is published:** FDA's consumer education pages — *"How to Understand and Use the Nutrition Facts Label"* [S1] and the dedicated page *"The Lows and Highs of Percent Daily Value on the Nutrition Facts Label"* [S3], plus the matching downloadable FDA consumer fact sheet.

**Critical nuance almost every blog gets wrong:** the 5/20 rule is **FDA consumer education guidance, not a codified regulation**. It does not appear in 21 CFR 101.9 as a binding threshold [S3][S5]. It is FDA's own plain-language heuristic, which is exactly why we can teach it with confidence — but we must describe it as FDA's *general guide*, never as "the law says." **[HIGH]** on the wording and publication location; **[MEDIUM]** on the assertion that it is nowhere codified as a consumer threshold (it is, however, echoed in the proposed front-of-package rule's Low/Med/High cutoffs — see Section G).

**The 5/20 rule cuts both ways and the product must teach both directions:**
- For nutrients to **limit** (saturated fat, sodium, added sugars): you generally want the low side.
- For nutrients to **get more of** (fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium): you generally want the high side [S1].

## B3. Why %DV is missing on some lines — and what to do instead **[HIGH]**

| Line | %DV shown? | Why | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total Sugars** | No | FDA has not established a Daily Value for total sugars [S1][S52]. | Read the indented **Added Sugars** line under it, which does have a %DV. |
| **Trans Fat** | No | Expert bodies did not provide a reference value FDA considered sufficient to set a DV [S52]. | Compare grams — and read the **ingredient list** for "partially hydrogenated oil." See Section H. |
| **Protein** | Usually no | Voluntary for general-population foods; required only if a protein claim is made or the food is for children under 4 [S5]. | Compare grams. See Section E. |
| **Vitamin A / Vitamin C** | Often absent entirely | No longer mandatory [S4]. | Nothing to do; their absence is not a red flag. |

# C. SERVING SIZE REALITY

## C1. RACC — Reference Amounts Customarily Consumed **[HIGH]**
**What it is:** RACCs are FDA-set values representing the amount of a food **customarily consumed at one eating occasion**. Manufacturers use the RACC for their product category to derive the serving size printed on the panel. The RACC lists live in Tables 1 and 2 of 21 CFR 101.12(b): **Table 1** covers foods for infants and children 1 through 3 years; **Table 2** covers the general population, age 4 and older [S7][S40].

**What it is NOT:** a recommendation of how much to eat. FDA states this plainly — by law, serving sizes must be based on the amount of food people **typically** consume, not how much they **should** consume [S6][S41].

**Where the numbers came from:** the original RACCs were built primarily on national food consumption surveys from 1977–1978 and 1987–1988. The 2016 rule updated them using **2003–2008 NHANES** data — which is why serving sizes for things like ice cream and soda got bigger, not because FDA endorsed eating more, but because it was documenting what people actually do [S7][S41].

**Note the RACC/serving-size distinction:** RACC is the regulatory reference amount; the **serving size** printed on the label is the household measure that most closely approximates the RACC for that product [S7].

## C2. The single-serving container rule **[HIGH]**
> A product that is packaged and sold individually and that contains **less than 200 percent** of the applicable reference amount must be considered a **single-serving container**, and the **entire content** must be labeled as one serving. [S5][S7]

**The 150%–200% wrinkle [MEDIUM]:** for a product packaged and sold individually containing **more than 150 percent and less than 200 percent** of the RACC, the label may **voluntarily** add a second column showing amounts and %DVs per the household measure that most closely approximates the RACC [S5][S7]. (Under the *pre-2016* regulation, such products could instead be labeled as 1 **or** 2 servings at the manufacturer's discretion where the RACC was 100 g / 100 mL or larger — that discretion is the origin of the old "20 oz bottle = 2.5 servings" absurdity that the current rule eliminated [S7].)

**Why this is the single most valuable fact in Section C for a family:** it is why a 20 oz bottle of soda now shows one set of numbers for the whole bottle. Under the old rules it showed 2.5 servings, and shoppers systematically under-read their intake.

## C3. Dual-column labeling — when it is required **[HIGH]**
> Products containing **at least 200 percent and up to and including 300 percent** of the applicable RACC — i.e. amounts that could reasonably be consumed at a single eating occasion — must carry **dual-column labeling**: one column "per serving" and one column "per container" (or per unit) [S5][S7].

Put plainly for the product: **the 2-to-3 servings rule.** If the package holds roughly 2 to 3 servings, the law assumes a person might eat the whole thing, so the label must show you both numbers.

| Package size relative to RACC | What the label must do |
|---|---|
| Less than 150% of RACC | Single-serving container; whole package = 1 serving [S5] |
| More than 150% and less than 200% of RACC | Still a single-serving container; **optional** extra column at the RACC amount [S5][S7] |
| **At least 200% up to and including 300% of RACC** | **Dual-column labeling required** (per serving AND per container/unit) [S5][S7] |
| More than 300% of RACC | Ordinary per-serving labeling; dual column not required |

**Exemptions [MEDIUM]:** dual-column requirements do not apply to certain products — for example those requiring further preparation, those commonly consumed in combination with other foods, and certain categories such as popcorn, where alternative formats are permitted [S7]. Verify the exemption list at [S7] before writing any absolute statement in the parent guide.

## C4. The 10-second serving-size read (product-ready)
1. **Servings per container** — is this 1, or is it 8?
2. **Serving size** — is it a realistic amount of what is actually going in the bowl?
3. If there are **two columns**, the right-hand one is the whole package. That is the honest number if the package gets finished.

# D. ADDED SUGARS

## D1. Definition **[HIGH]**
Added sugars include:
- sugars added during the **processing** of foods (e.g., sucrose, dextrose),
- foods packaged **as sweeteners** (e.g., table sugar),
- sugars from **syrups and honey**,
- sugars from **concentrated fruit or vegetable juices** in excess of what would be expected from the same volume of 100% juice of the same type [S8].

They **do not** include naturally occurring sugars found in **milk, fruits, and vegetables** [S8].

## D2. Why it was added to the label **[HIGH]**
FDA required the added-sugars declaration because excess added-sugar consumption makes it difficult to meet nutrient needs within calorie limits, and because adding sugars to foods increases calories without contributing important nutrients [S8]. In short: it is a **nutrient-density** line, not a morality line. Say it that way in the product.

## D3. The Daily Value and the teaspoon math **[HIGH]**

| Item | Value | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| Added Sugars DV (adults & children 4+) | **50 g/day** | 10% of a 2,000-calorie diet = 200 kcal; 200 kcal ÷ 4 kcal/g = **50 g** [S8] |
| Grams per teaspoon (label-teaching standard) | **4 g ≈ 1 tsp** | Granulated sugar is ~4.2 g/tsp; FDA and consumer educators use the round 4 g for teaching [S8, S51] |
| 50 g DV expressed in teaspoons | **12.5 tsp** | 50 ÷ 4 = 12.5 |

**The exact math to print in the workbook:**
> grams of added sugar ÷ 4 = teaspoons (approximately)

**Worked conversions for the product (use these exact numbers):**

| Added sugars on label | ÷ 4 = teaspoons | %DV (of 50 g) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 g | 0.5 tsp | 4% |
| 4 g | 1 tsp | 8% |
| 5 g | 1.25 tsp | 10% |
| 10 g | 2.5 tsp | 20% |
| 12 g | 3 tsp | 24% |
| 20 g | 5 tsp | 40% |
| 25 g | 6.25 tsp | 50% |
| 39 g | ~9.75 tsp | 78% |
| 50 g | 12.5 tsp | 100% |

**Accuracy note to preserve credibility:** because a teaspoon of granulated sugar is about **4.2 g**, dividing by 4 slightly *over*states teaspoons. State in the parent guide: *"We use 4 grams per teaspoon because it is the standard teaching conversion and the arithmetic is doable in your head. The precise figure is about 4.2 grams, so the real teaspoon count is a touch lower."* That one sentence is the difference between a product a dietitian respects and one they do not. **[HIGH]** on 4.2 g; **[MEDIUM]** on FDA specifically endorsing 4 g as its conversion.

## D4. Recommended added-sugar limits for children **[HIGH]**

**American Heart Association (scientific statement, *Circulation*, 2016; widely restated by AAP) [S9][S10]:**

| Age group | AHA recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Under 2 years** | **No added sugars.** |
| **2 through 18 years** | **Less than 6 teaspoons (about 25 g, about 100 calories) of added sugars per day.** |
| Sugar-sweetened beverages, 2–18 | **No more than one 8-ounce serving per week.** |

**AAP:** AAP News reported and endorsed the AHA guidance under the headline "AHA: Limit children's sugar consumption to 6 teaspoons per day" [S10]. AAP's own related guidance limits 100% fruit juice by age: **none before 12 months**; **at most 4 oz/day for ages 1–3**; **4–6 oz/day for ages 4–6**; **at most 8 oz/day for ages 7–18** [S32].

**US Dietary Guidelines for Americans — THIS CHANGED IN 2026, READ CAREFULLY [MEDIUM→see gaps]:**
The **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans** were released **January 7, 2026** by HHS and USDA [S33][S34]. On added sugars they represent a substantive revision: the guidance shifts from a **daily percentage-based limit** (previously: under 10% of total daily calories for ages 2+) to a **per-meal quantitative limit**, recommends **avoiding added sugars during infancy and early childhood**, and limits intake to **no more than 10 g of added sugars per meal** thereafter — roughly 2.5 teaspoons per meal [S35][S36][S37]. Multiple analyses state the "avoid" recommendation now extends **from birth through 10 years of age**, up from the previous under-2 threshold [S35][S36][S37]. The DGA also states that **"no amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended or considered part of a healthy or nutritious diet"** [S36].

**Two cautions the product must respect:**
1. **The DGA change does not change the label.** The Added Sugars **Daily Value is still 50 g** and the %DV on every package is still calculated from 50 g [S2][S8]. Changing a Daily Value requires FDA rulemaking, which has not occurred. Any product copy implying the panel now reflects the new DGA is **false**.
2. **The exact age threshold is contested in this pass.** See CONFIDENCE & GAPS — some HHS-sourced summaries described the "avoid" guidance as applying to children **4 and under**, while peer-reviewed and university summaries state **through age 10** [S34] vs [S35][S36][S37]. **Do not print an age number** until a human confirms against the DGA document itself [S33].
3. **Tone.** A "no added sugar until 10" headline in a kids' product is exactly the kind of message that can slide into restriction and shame. See Section I. Our product reports what the guidelines say; it does not police any family's kitchen.

**Context for parents [HIGH]:** in 2017–2018, average added-sugar intake for Americans aged 2–19 was about **17 teaspoons per day** [S27]. That is roughly 68 g — well above both the AHA figure and the label's 50 g DV.

## E2. Sodium specifics **[HIGH]**
- DV = **2,300 mg/day** [S2]. CDC states the recommended daily limit is 2,300 mg for ages 14 and older [S28].
- **About 9 in 10 US children consume more sodium than recommended**; US children aged 6–18 average roughly **3,300 mg/day** before salt added at the table [S29].
- Most sodium is not from the shaker — it comes pre-loaded in packaged and restaurant foods [S29]. This is the whole argument for label literacy, and it is the strongest evidence-backed hook in the product.

## E3. Dietary fiber specifics **[HIGH]**
- DV = **28 g/day**, raised from the pre-2016 value of **25 g/day** [S2][S13].
- **This DV change silently moved the claim thresholds.** Because "good source" and "high" are defined as *percentages* of the DV (see Section G), the gram cutoffs for fiber claims moved when the DV moved:

| Claim | Rule | Under the OLD 25 g DV | Under the CURRENT 28 g DV |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Good source of fiber" | 10–19% DV | 2.5 g – 4.9 g | **2.8 g – 5.5 g** |
| "High fiber" / "Excellent source of fiber" | 20% DV or more | 5 g or more | **5.6 g or more** |

Many consumer articles and even some nutrition handouts still quote the old **2.5 g / 5 g** figures. Our product will quote the current figures and will say why they changed. **[HIGH]** on the percentage rule [S13]; **[MEDIUM-HIGH]** on the recalculated gram figures (arithmetic is certain; confirm FDA has not published a transition accommodation).

## E4. Saturated fat specifics **[HIGH]**
- DV = **20 g/day** [S2].
- 5/20 applied: **1 g or less = low; 4 g or more = high** per serving.
- Note for the parent guide: the 2025–2030 DGA retained a saturated-fat limit framed around **less than 10% of calories per day** while simultaneously expanding recommendations for full-fat dairy, a tension multiple commentators flagged [S36]. Report the tension; do not adjudicate it.

## E5. Protein — why the %DV is almost always blank **[HIGH]**
- DV = **50 g/day** [S2].
- **%DV for protein is voluntary** on foods for the general population aged 4 and older. It becomes **mandatory** only when (a) a **protein claim** is made on the package (e.g., "high protein," "excellent source of protein"), or (b) the food is represented or purported to be for **infants or children under 4 years** [S5].
- **Why manufacturers skip it:** calculating protein %DV legally requires correcting for protein **quality**, using the **PDCAAS** (Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score). PDCAAS requires laboratory analysis of the food's essential amino acid profile plus a digestibility factor — an expensive test with no upside if you are not making a protein claim. So the line is left blank [S5].
- **The teachable consequence:** a blank protein %DV means nothing about the food's quality. It means the manufacturer did not pay for the test. Kill the myth that a blank means "no protein value."

# F. INGREDIENT LIST LITERACY

## F1. The descending-by-weight rule **[HIGH]**
> Ingredients must be listed by common or usual name **in descending order of predominance by weight** [S12].

Weight is measured **as the ingredient is added during manufacturing**, not in the finished product after cooking or moisture loss [S12].

**Two exceptions that matter [MEDIUM]:**
1. **The 2% rule.** Ingredients present at **2 percent or less by weight** need not be in descending order, provided they appear at the end of the list after a qualifying statement such as "Contains 2% or less of..." [S12].
2. **Fat/oil blends.** Where a blend of fats and/or oils is used and the manufacturer varies the mixture, the parenthetical listing need not be in descending order [S12].

**The literacy skill to teach:** the first three ingredients are most of the food. And a manufacturer can split one sweetener across several names — sugar, corn syrup, honey, molasses — so that no single one rises to the top of the list, even though sweeteners collectively dominate. That is legal, and it is exactly why the **Added Sugars line** (Section D) was created: it re-aggregates what the ingredient list disaggregates. This is the single best "aha" in Section F.

## F2. The many names for added sugar **[MEDIUM]**
There is no official FDA master list of sugar synonyms. The list below is compiled from UCSF SugarScience's "Hidden in Plain Sight" project and widely circulated extension-service handouts enumerating 60+ names [S51]. Treat the **list membership** as [MEDIUM] and the **concept** as [HIGH].

| Category | Names you will see on ingredient lists |
|---|---|
| Obvious sugars | sugar, brown sugar, raw sugar, granulated sugar, white sugar, table sugar, powdered sugar, icing sugar, confectioners' sugar, superfine sugar, golden sugar, caster sugar |
| "-ose" chemical names | sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose, maltose, lactose, galactose, mannose, ribose, trehalose |
| Corn-derived | corn syrup, corn syrup solids, high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), crystalline fructose, isoglucose, glucose syrup, dextran, maltodextrin |
| Syrups | maple syrup, agave nectar/syrup, rice syrup, brown rice syrup, rice malt, malt syrup, golden syrup, refiners' syrup, carob syrup, sorghum syrup, tapioca syrup, pancake syrup, treacle, king's syrup, invert syrup |
| Cane-derived, "wholesome"-sounding | evaporated cane juice, dehydrated cane juice, evaporated cane syrup, cane crystals, sucanat, panela, rapadura, muscovado, turbinado, demerara, jaggery, piloncillo, panocha |
| Fruit-derived | fruit juice concentrate, fruit juice crystals, grape juice concentrate, apple juice concentrate, date sugar, date syrup |
| Malt & dairy | barley malt, malted barley, malt extract, malt sugar, malted milk, ethyl maltol |
| Honey & molasses | honey, molasses, blackstrap molasses |

Mark as a **heuristic**, not a rule — maltodextrin and dextran do not end in -ose, and lactose in plain milk is not an added sugar.

**Critical honesty check for the product:** a name-spotting game is fun, but the **Added Sugars grams line is the authoritative number** [S8]. Teach the game as the ingredient-list skill and the grams line as the decision. Never let the game imply that the presence of any sugar name is itself a verdict on a food.

## F3. How "whole grain" claims work **[MEDIUM]**
- **FDA has never issued a binding regulation defining "whole grain" for labeling.** FDA issued **draft guidance in 2006** on whole grain label statements; it has not been superseded by a final binding rule [S47].
- **"Multigrain"** means several grains. It says **nothing** about whether any of them are whole [S48].
- **"Made with whole grain"** means whole grain is present. It does not say how much, and it is frequently a minority of the grain content [S48].
- **The Whole Grain Stamp** is an industry certification from the **Whole Grains Council**, not an FDA program. Its basic stamp standard is that at least **51%** of the grain ingredients are whole grain; a "100%" stamp indicates all grain ingredients are whole grain [S48]. **[MEDIUM]** on the 51% figure — confirm current stamp criteria at [S48] before printing.
- **What to actually look for:** the **first ingredient**. If the first ingredient reads "whole wheat flour," "whole grain oats," "whole grain corn," etc., whole grain is the predominant grain by weight [S12][S48]. If it reads "enriched wheat flour" or "wheat flour," that is refined flour regardless of a "made with whole grain" flag on the front.
- **Enriched vs. whole:** "enriched" means nutrients removed in refining have been partially added back. It is not a synonym for whole grain. **[MEDIUM]**

## F4. Allergen declaration rules **[HIGH]**
- The US currently recognizes **nine (9) major food allergens**: **milk, egg, fish, Crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, and sesame** [S11].
- **Sesame is included.** It became the ninth major food allergen under the **FASTER Act**, signed **April 23, 2021**, with labeling and all other major-allergen requirements applying **as of January 1, 2023** [S11].
- Food already in distribution before January 1, 2023 was not required to be pulled, so a transition period existed where some packages did not list sesame [S11].
- **A consequence parents should know [MEDIUM]:** after the sesame requirement took effect, some manufacturers **intentionally added sesame** to products that previously contained none — and labeled it — rather than implement cross-contact controls. This kept them compliant while reducing safe options for sesame-allergic consumers. FDA responded by updating draft guidance on allergen cross-contact (announced September 26, 2023) [S46].
- Declaration is made either in the ingredient list (with the food source name in parentheses) or in a separate **"Contains"** statement immediately after or adjacent to the ingredient list. **[MEDIUM]** — confirm formatting details at [S11] before writing prescriptive copy.
- **"May contain" / "processed in a facility with" statements are voluntary and unregulated in content.** Do not teach families to read them as a graded risk scale. **[MEDIUM]**

# G. FRONT-OF-PACKAGE CLAIMS — THE MARKETING MINEFIELD

## G1. Legally defined nutrient content claims **[HIGH]** on the framework; per-row confidence noted

General principle: nutrient content claims are defined in **21 CFR Part 101, Subpart D** (§§101.54–101.69), with general principles at **§101.13**. Thresholds are assessed **per RACC and per labeled serving** (and, for foods with small RACCs — 30 g or less, or 2 tablespoons or less — also per 50 g) [S13][S15][S55].

| Claim | Legal threshold | Cite / confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Free** (calorie) | Less than 5 calories per RACC and per labeled serving | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Low calorie** | 40 calories or less per RACC | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Fat free** | **Less than 0.5 g** total fat per RACC and per labeled serving | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **Low fat** | **3 g or less** total fat per RACC | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **Saturated fat free** | Less than 0.5 g saturated fat **and** less than 0.5 g trans fat per RACC | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Low saturated fat** | **1 g or less** saturated fat per RACC **and** no more than **15% of calories** from saturated fat | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **Cholesterol free** | Less than 2 mg cholesterol and 2 g or less saturated fat per RACC | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Low cholesterol** | **20 mg or less** cholesterol and 2 g or less saturated fat per RACC | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Sodium free** | Less than 5 mg sodium per RACC | [S55] [MEDIUM] |
| **Very low sodium** | **35 mg or less** sodium per RACC | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **Low sodium** | **140 mg or less** sodium per RACC | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **Sugar free** | **Less than 0.5 g** sugars per RACC and per labeled serving, with no ingredient that is a sugar or generally understood to contain sugars | [S55] [HIGH] |
| **"Reduced" / "Less" / "Fewer"** (any nutrient) | **At least 25% less** of that nutrient than an appropriate reference food; the reference food and the % difference must be declared adjacent to the most prominent claim | [S13][S15] [HIGH] |
| **Light / Lite** | If the food derives **50% or more of calories from fat**: fat reduced by **50% or more** vs. reference. If it derives **less than 50% of calories from fat**: either calories reduced by **at least one-third**, or fat reduced by **50% or more**. "Light in sodium" = sodium reduced by **50% or more** | [S14][S55] [HIGH] |
| **Good source of / Contains / Provides** | **10% to 19% of the DV** per RACC | [S13] [HIGH] |
| **High / Rich in / Excellent source of** | **20% or more of the DV** per RACC | [S13] [HIGH] |
| **More / Added / Extra / Plus / Fortified / Enriched** | **At least 10% of the DV more** than the reference food | [S13] [MEDIUM] |
| **"No added sugar" / "No sugar added"** | No sugars or sugar-containing ingredients added during processing or packing; the product must not contain ingredients whose function is to add sweetness; if the food is not low- or reduced-calorie, the label **must** state that it is not low calorie or calorie reduced | [S55] [MEDIUM] |

**The "good source / high" trap worth a whole spread in the parent guide:** these two claims are **percentages of the DV**, so their gram equivalents move whenever a DV moves. The clearest live example is fiber (Section E3): "high fiber" meant 5 g under the old 25 g DV and now means **5.6 g** under the 28 g DV [S13]. Anyone quoting 5 g today is quoting a retired number.

## G2. Claims with NO legal definition — the real minefield **[HIGH]**

| Term | Legal status |
|---|---|
| **"Natural"** | **FDA has never engaged in rulemaking to define it.** FDA operates a longstanding informal **policy**: nothing artificial or synthetic (including any color additive regardless of source) has been included in or added to a food that would not normally be expected to be there. That policy was **not** intended to address production methods (e.g., pesticide use) and does **not** address processing methods such as thermal technologies, pasteurization, or irradiation. FDA sought public comment; **as of 2026 there is still no formal binding definition** [S16]. |
| **"Lightly sweetened," "slightly sweet," "less sweet," "sorta sweet," "just a tad sweet"** | **No legal definition.** FDA has not defined "high sugar" or "low sugar" either. Litigation over these terms turns on whether a "reasonable consumer" would be misled, not on a threshold. Products carrying such terms have been documented containing 14–25 g of added sugar per serving — up to half the 50 g DV [S42]. |
| **"Multigrain"** | No FDA definition tied to whole grain content [S48]. |
| **"Made with whole grain"** | No binding quantity threshold; draft guidance only [S47][S48]. |
| **"Zero trans fat" / "0 g trans fat"** | Not a defined nutrient content claim; the "0 g" figure is a statement of the panel value, which is produced by a rounding rule. See Section H and G4 [S31][S52]. |
| **"Organic"** | **This one IS legally defined — but by USDA, not FDA.** See G3. |

## G3. Organic — USDA National Organic Program **[HIGH]**

| Label category | Requirement | USDA Organic seal allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| **"100 Percent Organic"** | 100% certified organic ingredients | Yes [S17] |
| **"Organic"** | **At least 95%** certified organic content; the remaining ≤5% must be non-organic substances allowed on the National List | Yes [S17] |
| **"Made with organic ___"** | **At least 70%** certified organic content; up to 30% allowed non-organic ingredients, all produced without GMOs | **No** [S17] |
| Below 70% organic | May identify organic ingredients only in the ingredient list | No [S17] |

**Teaching point:** organic is a *production-method* certification. It is not a nutrition claim and says nothing about added sugars, sodium, or calories. An organic cookie is a cookie.

## G4. "Zero trans fat" and the rounding loophole — exact threshold **[HIGH]**
> **If a serving contains less than 0.5 gram of trans fat, the content, when declared, must be expressed as "0 g."** [S31][S52]

That is the loophole, stated precisely. A product can legally print **0 g trans fat** while containing up to **0.49 g per serving**. Eat several servings and the zeros add up to a real number that never appeared anywhere on the package [S31].

**Two important refinements so the product is not alarmist:**
1. **The underlying source is largely gone.** FDA determined in **2015** that **partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs)** — the primary dietary source of artificial trans fat — are **not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS)**. Most uses had a June 18, 2018 cutoff, with a final compliance date extended to **January 1, 2021**; FDA completed further administrative actions removing PHO references from regulations in 2023 [S30]. So in 2026, industrially produced trans fat in US packaged food is far less prevalent than the "loophole" framing implies.
2. **Trans fat has not vanished entirely.** It occurs naturally in small amounts in meat and dairy from ruminant animals and at very low levels in some edible oils; FDA's PHO actions did not affect these natural sources [S30].
3. **The verification move to teach:** if the panel says 0 g trans fat, scan the ingredient list for **"partially hydrogenated"**. If that phrase is absent, the 0 is almost certainly a true zero.

## G5. CRITICAL — status of the updated "healthy" claim rule **[HIGH]**

| Item | Status as of 2026-09-18 |
|---|---|
| Final rule published | **December 27, 2024** (Federal Register) [S18] |
| Original effective date | February 25, 2025 |
| **Effective date delayed to** | **April 28, 2025** — HHS delayed it on February 24, 2025 under the incoming administration's regulatory freeze [S19] |
| **Compliance date** | **February 25, 2028** — unchanged by the delay [S18][S19] |
| Voluntary early use | Permitted from **April 28, 2025** [S19] |
| Current posture | **In force and phasing in.** Manufacturers may use the new criteria now; they must comply by Feb 25, 2028. Old-criteria "healthy" labels remain lawful until then. |

**What the new criteria require [MEDIUM]:** to bear "healthy," a food must (1) contain a specified minimum amount of a **food group equivalent (FGE)** — fruit, vegetables, whole grains, dairy, or protein foods — and (2) meet limits on **added sugars, sodium, and saturated fat** expressed as percentages of the DV, which **vary by food category**. The rule **removed** the old limits on **total fat** and **cholesterol** and **added** the added-sugars limit [S18]. Reported examples: mixed products must contain one total FGE with no more than **10% DV added sugars, 15% DV sodium, and 10% DV saturated fat** per RACC; baseline sodium for individual foods is no more than **10% DV** per RACC [S18]. **Do not publish the full FGE table** — it was not verified category by category.

**Practical consequence for shoppers [HIGH]:** under the new criteria, foods that previously could **not** say "healthy" now can — **nuts, seeds, higher-fat fish such as salmon, certain oils, and water**. Foods that previously could say "healthy" will **lose** the right to by Feb 25, 2028 — e.g. **fortified white bread, highly sweetened cereal, fortified fruit punch that is not 100% juice, and high-added-sugar fruit snacks** [S18].

**Product warning:** between now and February 2028 the word "healthy" on a package may reflect **either** standard. Teach families to ignore the word and read the panel. That is also the safest editorial position for us.

# H. THE ROUNDING RULES PARENTS NEVER HEAR ABOUT

This is the product's signature "aha." Get it exact. All rules below are from **21 CFR 101.9(c)** [S5], corroborated by multiple independent label-compliance restatements. Tagged **[MEDIUM-HIGH]** as a block: the arithmetic thresholds are consistently reported across sources, but the eCFR page could not be opened directly in this pass. **Have a human confirm this entire table against [S5] before print.**

| Line | Rounding increments | **Where it can legally become "0"** |
|---|---|---|
| **Calories** | Nearest **5-calorie** increment up to and including 50 cal; nearest **10-calorie** increment above 50 cal | **Less than 5 calories may be declared as 0** |
| **Total Fat** | Nearest **0.5 g** below 5 g; nearest **1 g** at/above 5 g | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"** |
| **Saturated Fat** | Nearest **0.5 g** below 5 g; nearest **1 g** above 5 g | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"** |
| **Trans Fat** | Nearest **0.5 g** below 5 g; nearest **1 g** above 5 g | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"** |
| **Cholesterol** | Nearest **5 mg** increment above 5 mg | **Less than 2 mg → "0 mg"**; 2–5 mg → "less than 5 mg" |
| **Sodium** | Nearest **5 mg** increment for 5–140 mg; nearest **10 mg** increment above 140 mg | **Less than 5 mg → "0 mg"** |
| **Total Carbohydrate** | Nearest **1 g** | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"**; less than 1 g may be "Contains less than 1 g" |
| **Dietary Fiber** | Nearest **1 g** | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"**; less than 1 g may be "Contains less than 1 g" |
| **Total Sugars** | Nearest **1 g** | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"**; less than 1 g may be "Contains less than 1 g" |
| **Added Sugars** | Nearest **1 g** | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"**; less than 1 g may be "Contains less than 1 g" |
| **Protein** | Nearest **1 g** | **Less than 0.5 g → "0 g"**; less than 1 g may be "Contains less than 1 g" |
| **Vitamins & minerals** | %DV to nearest **2%** up to 10%; nearest **5%** above 10% through 50%; nearest **10%** above 50% | **[MEDIUM] — verify at [S5]** |

### The three consequences to teach
1. **"0 g trans fat" can mean up to 0.49 g per serving** [S31]. Cross-check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated."
2. **"0 calories" can mean up to 4.9 calories per serving.** Combine this with a small serving size and a multi-serving container and small numbers compound.
3. **Serving size is the multiplier on every rounding rule.** A manufacturer that sets a smaller serving size gets smaller numbers *and* more opportunities to fall under a rounding floor. This is why Section C (serving size) must be taught **before** Section H (rounding) in every track.

### Why label numbers sometimes do not add up
Because each line rounds independently, the grams of fat + carbohydrate + protein multiplied by their calorie factors will often not exactly equal the printed Calories. This is normal and legal, not evidence of fraud. Teaching this pre-empts the conspiracy-flavored version of label literacy that circulates online. **[MEDIUM]**

# I. KID-SPECIFIC TRUTH — THE SECTION THAT PROTECTS THE CHILD AND THE BRAND

This section is **binding on every downstream writer**. Brief hard rule #2 lives here.

## I1. What AAP actually says about discussing food and weight with children **[HIGH]**

AAP's clinical report **"Preventing Obesity and Eating Disorders in Adolescents"** (*Pediatrics*, 2016;138(3):e20161649; **reaffirmed June 2022**) makes these recommendations [S23]:

| AAP recommendation | Verbatim thrust |
|---|---|
| **Avoid weight talk** | Encourage families **not to talk about weight** but rather to talk about **healthy eating and being active to stay healthy** [S23] |
| **Discourage dieting** | Discourage **dieting, skipping meals, and diet pills**; instead encourage and support healthy eating and physical activity behaviors **that can be maintained on an ongoing basis** [S23] |
| **Focus on habits, not weight** | The focus should be on **healthy living and healthy habits** rather than on weight [S23] |
| **Promote positive body image** | Promote positive body image; **do not** encourage body dissatisfaction or use it as a motivator for dieting [S23] |
| **Encourage family meals** | More frequent family meals [S23] |
| **Key reassurance** | Evidence suggests obesity prevention and treatment, **conducted correctly**, do not predispose to eating disorders [S23] |

AAP-affiliated consumer guidance reinforces: **avoid labeling foods "good" or "bad"** in front of a child; **avoid commenting on their body or anyone else's**; **avoid using food as a reward or punishment**; and teach children to use **hunger and fullness cues** rather than external triggers like the amount of food on the plate [S49]. AAP also publishes a companion clinical report on **Identification and Management of Eating Disorders in Children and Adolescents** (*Pediatrics*, 2021;147(1):e2020040279) [S24].

## I2. Evidence that restriction and food moralizing backfire **[HIGH]**

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Restricting access **focuses children's attention** on the restricted food and **increases their desire** to obtain and consume it — the "forbidden fruit" effect | [S26] |
| Children show **increased intake when restrictions are lifted**; the effect is more robust for girls and for children with lower inhibitory control | [S26] |
| In Birch & Fisher's work with 7-year-old girls, girls restricted at home **not only ate more** of the snack foods, they had **negative feelings about their own eating** | [S25] |
| Maternal use of controlling and restrictive feeding practices **impairs children's ability to self-regulate energy intake** and encourages **eating in the absence of hunger** | [S25] |
| High restriction at age 5 **predicted eating in the absence of hunger** at ages 7–9 | [S25] |
| Dieting — caloric restriction for weight loss — is a risk factor for **both** obesity and eating disorders; dieting behaviors were associated with roughly **twofold increased risk of becoming overweight** and **1.5-fold increased risk of binge eating** at 5-year follow-up | [S23][S45] |
| School obesity-prevention programs **may inadvertently reinforce behaviors that lead to eating disorders** | [S45] |

**The direct product implication:** a label-literacy product that is really a restriction product will make the outcome worse and will be indefensible if challenged. Our product teaches **reading**, and reading only.

## I3. Age-appropriate framing — what a 6-year-old can grasp vs. a 12-year-old **[MEDIUM]**

Grounded in standard cognitive-development staging [S50a]. Treat the stage ages as approximate; children vary.

| | **Ages 6–9** (concrete operational, roughly 7–11) | **Ages 10–13** (transitioning to formal operational, roughly 12+) |

The Ages 10–13 tier teaches **comparing and questioning** (which is more, and why does the front of the box say something different). **Neither tier teaches a child a personal intake target.**

## I4. What a product like this MUST avoid **[HIGH]**

|---|---|
| Call foods "good," "bad," "clean," "junk," "healthy," "unhealthy" | Describe what a food **provides** ("this one has more fiber"), or say "everyday foods" and "sometimes foods" only if the parent track explains that all foods fit |

| Use before/after bodies, cartoon "fat" villains, or any body-shape imagery as a stand-in for food quality | Use packages, magnifying glasses, detective and decoder motifs. The product's visual antagonist is **confusing marketing**, never a body |

# J. TWELVE MYTH VS. FACT ENTRIES

| # | **MYTH** | **FACT** | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "The serving size is how much I'm supposed to eat." | Serving sizes are legally required to be based on the amount people **typically** consume, not how much they **should**. It is a comparison unit, not a recommendation. | [S6][S41] |
| 2 | "0 g trans fat means there's no trans fat." | Anything **under 0.5 g per serving** must be declared as "0 g." Several servings of several such foods add up to trans fat that never appeared on any label. Check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated oil." | [S31][S52] |
| 3 | "%DV is calculated for my kid." | Every %DV on an ordinary grocery package is calculated for **adults and children 4 and older on a 2,000-calorie diet**. FDA publishes separate Daily Values for children 1–3, infants, and pregnancy/lactation — but those only appear on foods marketed to those groups. | [S2][S5] |
| 4 | "Total Sugars tells me how much sugar was added." | Total Sugars includes naturally occurring sugars in milk, fruit, and vegetables. The **Added Sugars** line — indented beneath it, with a %DV — is the one that isolates what was put in. | [S1][S8] |
| 5 | "Blank protein %DV means the food has no useful protein." | Protein %DV is **voluntary** for general-population foods; it is required only when a protein claim is made or the food is for children under 4. Calculating it requires a PDCAAS lab test most manufacturers skip. The blank means no test, not no protein. | [S5] |
| 6 | "'Natural' is regulated." | FDA has **never made a rule defining "natural."** It operates only an informal policy — and that policy explicitly does not address production methods like pesticide use, or processing methods like pasteurization and irradiation. As of 2026 there is still no binding definition. | [S16] |
| 7 | "'Lightly sweetened' means low in sugar." | There is **no legal definition** of "lightly sweetened" — or of "low sugar" or "high sugar." Products using such terms have carried 14–25 g of added sugar per serving, up to half the 50 g Daily Value. | [S42] |
| 8 | "'Multigrain' and 'made with whole grain' mean whole grain." | "Multigrain" means several grains, whole or not. "Made with whole grain" means some whole grain is present, with no required amount. FDA has issued only **draft** guidance, never a binding definition. Check whether a **whole grain is the first ingredient**. | [S47][S48][S12] |
| 9 | "There are 8 major food allergens in the US." | There are **nine**. Sesame became the ninth under the FASTER Act, with labeling required as of **January 1, 2023**. | [S11] |
| 10 | "If sugar isn't near the top of the ingredient list, there isn't much." | Ingredients are ordered by weight, so a manufacturer can split sweeteners across several names — sugar, corn syrup, honey, fruit juice concentrate — and keep each below the top. That is legal. The **Added Sugars grams line** re-aggregates what the ingredient list splits apart. | [S12][S8] |
| 11 | "If a package says 'healthy,' FDA has verified it's good for you." | "Healthy" is a defined nutrient content claim that is **mid-transition**. FDA's updated rule was published Dec 27, 2024, took effect April 28, 2025, and is not mandatory until **February 25, 2028**. Until then a package may be using the **old** criteria. Foods like highly sweetened cereal and non-juice fruit punch will lose the claim; nuts, salmon, certain oils, and water gain it. | [S18][S19] |
| 12 | "Front-of-package 'Low sodium!' style boxes are already required by FDA." | Not yet. FDA **proposed** a mandatory front-of-package "Nutrition Info" box on **January 16, 2025** (Low/Med/High for saturated fat, sodium, added sugars). The comment period closed **July 15, 2025**. As of September 2026 **no final rule has issued**; it remains on FDA's 2026 priority list. | [S20][S21][S22] |

**The 10-second read:**
1. **Servings:** ~8. Numbers are per 1 1/2 cups — a realistic bowl, so no mental multiplication needed.
2. **Calories:** 140.
3. **Nutrients to limit:** sodium 8% (mid), saturated fat 3% (**low**, under 5%), added sugars 4% (**low**).
4. **Nutrients to get more of:** fiber 14% (mid, not "high" — it would need 20% / 5.6 g), iron 45% (**high**), vitamin D and calcium 10% each.
5. **Added sugars in teaspoons:** 2 ÷ 4 = **0.5 tsp**.
6. **Ingredient check:** whole grain oats listed first = whole grain is the predominant grain [S12].
7. **Verdict language for the product:** *"Low in added sugars, a solid iron number, fiber is middling. Sodium is the line worth noticing — cereal is a quiet sodium source."* **No good/bad verdict.**
